Is the Veda authorless, or authored by Īśvara?
Both answers are āstika. Both defend the Veda as pramāṇa. They deal with nuance.

A thread on apauruṣeyatva — Mīmāṃsā vs Nyāya vs Advaita. 🧵

The question is not “is the Veda old.” It is: does a puruṣa stand behind its anupūrvī, its fixed word-order?

If yes, human defect can enter it. If no, it cannot.
Apauruṣeyatva is an argument about error, not antiquity.

वेदोऽनित्यः पौरुषेयश्च

The pūrvapakṣa (for Mīmāmsakas) — Nyāya and the Bauddhas: वेदोऽनित्यः पौरुषेयश्च।

Begin from ordinary speech: उत्पन्नो गकारः, नष्टो गकारः — the sound ‘g’ arose, the sound ‘g’ perished. Even the illiterate say it.

Varṇas perish → padas perish → the Veda perishes.

Second, the śākhā names. Kāṭhaka, Kālāpa, Kauthuma, Paippalāda, Taittirīya — all formed from personal names.

Pāṇini licenses that taddhita only where something is प्रोक्तम् or कृतम्: spoken, made.

Grammar itself registers the Veda as a made thing.

Third, the Veda names mortals. बबरः प्रावाहणिरकामयत, कुरुविन्द औद्दालकिरकामयत।

Auddālaki = son of Uddālaka. But then, before Auddālaki was born, this passage did not exist.

A text carrying birth and death in it cannot be beginningless.

Fourth, absurdity.

वनस्पतयः सत्रमासत, सर्पाः सत्रमासत — trees sat in sacrificial session; snakes sat in sacrificial session.

Trees and snakes performing a sattra? That is the speech of a madman or a child. Human authorship explains it; eternality does not.

The inference that carries the pūrvapakṣa: विमतं वेदवाक्यं पौरुषेयम्, वाक्यत्वात्, कालिदासादिवाक्यवत्।

The Veda is authored, because it consists of sentences — like Kālidāsa’s.

The missing author?

So with an old well: nobody recalls who dug it. Memory fails from अनादर.

Nyāya adds śruti on its own side: त्रयो वेदा अजायन्त — the three Vedas were born, Ṛg from Agni, Yajus from Vāyu, Sāman from Āditya.

The position is summarised in the Nyāyakusumāñjali, and worked out at length in the Nyāyamañjarī, Śāstradīpikā and Pramāṇavārttika.

And the positive Nyāya thesis, as Vedāntaparibhāṣā reports it:

वेदानां नित्यसर्वज्ञपरमेश्वरप्रणीतत्वेन प्रामाण्यम्।

The Veda is valid because its author is an eternal, omniscient Īśvara. Authority flows from the speaker’s āptatva.

मीमांसामतम्

So the real axis is now visible.

Nyāya: a text is reliable when its author is reliable.

Mīmāṃsā: a text is reliable when there is no author who could be unreliable.

Same conclusion about the Veda.

Different epistemologies.

Mīmāṃsā’s reply starts from the nature of vedārtha.

Dharma is alaukika and apūrva. It cannot be placed in any human buddhi to begin with — and what a man has never conceived, he cannot compose.

Authorship fails before it starts.

On the śākhā names: समाख्या follows pravacana, not racanā.

Vaiśampāyana is remembered as knowing every śākhā; Kaṭha taught only this one. Among many-śākhā scholars, the man who transmits one distinctively lends it his name.

The name marks a teacher, not a maker.

On प्रावाहणि: no person named Pravāhaṇa is attested anywhere.

Derive it instead from प्र + वह् + इ — प्रावाहणः, that which drives forward. बबर is अनुकृतिशब्द, an imitative sound.

Both words then denote Vāyu. An eternal referent, not a dead man.

On trees performing a sattra: these are arthavāda, स्तुतिपर.

If even insentient trees are spoken of as sitting in session, how much more the learned brāhmaṇas.

Praise does not falsify injunction. अग्निहोत्रं जुहुयात् स्वर्गकामः stands untouched.

Then the counter-inference. वाक्यत्वात् is met by सत्प्रतिपक्ष:

वेदः प्रागभावप्रतियोगितावच्छेदकधर्माभाववान् अस्मर्यमाणकर्तृकत्वात् आत्मादिवत्।

And वाक्यत्व is itself सोपाधिक — the upādhi being जन्यज्ञानमूलकत्व. It proves nothing on its own.

On the well: a bad analogy.

Even there we remember in general that someone dug it, and the disregard is visible.

The Veda is revered by everyone, and still no author is recalled. Universal reverence plus total absence of memory points one way only.

And “forgotten through disregard” cannot be said of the ṛṣis who fixed the pada and krama recitations, who composed the smṛtis derived from the Veda, who were sarvajña.

To call them careless about the Veda’s author is अतिसाहस — sheer recklessness.

Two supporting inferences: पूर्वकालो न वेदशून्यः, कालत्वात्, वर्तमानकालवत्।

प्राक्कालिकं वेदाध्ययनं गुर्वध्ययनपूर्वकम्, अध्ययनत्वात्, अद्यतनाध्ययनवत्।

Every study of the Veda presupposes a teacher’s earlier study. The regress has no first term.

Perhaps the sharpest argument:

Those who posit an author cannot agree who he was.

Some say Īśvara, some Hiraṇyagarbha, some Prajāpati, some many authors.

Nobody disputes the author of the Mānava, the Bhārata, or the Buddhist texts. The disagreement is itself the symptom

On अग्नेर्ऋग्वेदः: read ज्ञापित, not जनित. Agni revealed it, did not generate it.

अनादिनिधना नित्या वागुत्सृष्टा स्वयंभुवा। शिवाद्या ऋषिपर्यन्ताः स्मर्तारोऽस्य न कारकाः॥

From Śiva down to the ṛṣis: rememberers of the Veda, not makers of it.

Finally the vikalpa: who is this author?

A man? Knowledge of dharma comes only from the Veda.

A yogin? Dharma is beyond the outer senses, and manas cannot reach it either.

Īśvara? अन्योन्याश्रय — the Veda establishes Īśvara, and Īśvara composes the Veda.

A smaller barb: why would an author describe his own dhyāna in his own text?

Mīmāṃsā’s definitions:
अपौरुषेयत्व = same-order transmission through all past time, universally revered, no remembered author. नित्यत्व = counterpositive of neither prior absence nor destruction.

अद्वैतमतम्

Now Advaita — and the surprise is that it concedes half the pūrvapakṣa.

अस्माकं तु मते वेदो न नित्यः, उत्पत्तिमत्त्वात्।

Because śruti says so: Ṛg, Yajus, Sāman and Atharvan are the out-breath of that Great Being.

But not momentary either.

“The very Veda Devadatta studied, I study too.” “This is that same ग.”

Pratyabhijñā rules out क्षणिकत्व. So the Veda arises at sṛṣṭi and perishes at pralaya, like ākāśa — the varṇas do not flicker in and out meanwhile.

Why unmanifest when unspoken, then?

Because the vyañjaka is absent. Utterance manifests varṇas; it does not manufacture them.

अन्धकारस्थघटानुपलम्भवत् — like a pot in a dark room, unseen but not destroyed.

So how can an originated, Īśvara-composed Veda still be apauruṣeya?

By tightening the term. Two candidate definitions fail.

“Uttered by a person”? Then even on the Guru’s view the Veda is pauruṣeya, through the teaching lineage.

“Originating in dependence on a person”? Then the Naiyāyika’s inference proves what we already grant — सिद्धसाधन.

What survives:

पौरुषेयत्वं = सजातीयोच्चारणानपेक्षोच्चारण-विषयत्वम्।

Being uttered without dependence on a prior utterance of the same kind.

At the start of each sarga, Īśvara composes a Veda whose anupūrvī matches the Veda established in the previous creation — not a different one.

The Bhārata has no such prior utterance behind it. Hence it is pauruṣeya. The Veda is not.

Summary

The three positions side by side:

Nyāya — anitya, pauruṣeya. Valid because its author is āpta.

Mīmāṃsā — nitya, apauruṣeya. No author, hence no defect.

Advaita — anitya, apauruṣeya. Valid because the word-order is repeated beginninglessly. • • •

shankara & nityatva

Shankaracharya’s Brahma Sūtra Bhāṣya (1.3.28) holds that śabda is nitya, eternal. The later Vedānta Paribhāṣā states that the Veda is not eternal. The two claims appear opposed. They are not — they operate at different levels of description, as this thread sets out. 🧵

The problem addressed in the Devatādhikaraṇa: if the word-referent relation is eternal, how is this sustained when Vedic words denote deities (Indra, Rudra) who are described as born and dying like humans? Does the referent’s impermanence undermine the word’s eternity?

Shankaracharya’s resolution distinguishes the individual (vyakti) from the universal or form (ākṛti). “Indra” does not denote a mortal being but a standing office, comparable to “commander-in-chief” (senāpati). Occupants change; the office persists.

A further question follows: what precisely is eternal about a word? Two positions are considered — Sphoṭavāda, which posits a distinct, partless sphoṭa manifested by sound, and Varṇavāda, which holds that the phonemes themselves are eternal.

Shankaracharya adopts Upavarṣa’s Varṇavāda. The same phoneme is recognized in each utterance (“so’yaṁ gakāraḥ”) through pratyabhijñā, a recognition not overturned by any stronger pramāṇa. This obviates the need to posit an additional, unobserved entity.

Sūtras 1.3.29–30 extend this account to the Veda as a whole, which recurs in identical sequence at each cosmic cycle (kalpa). This is termed pravāha-nityatva, eternity as an unbroken recurring stream, distinct from kūṭastha-nityatva, a single static entity.

Dharmarāja Adhvarīndra’s Vedānta Paribhāṣā

Centuries later, Dharmarāja Adhvarīndra’s Vedānta Paribhāṣā states: “vedo na nityaḥ, utpattimattvāt” — the Veda is not eternal, as it has an origin, arising at creation and dissolving at pralaya, in the manner of ākāśa and the other elements.

The Paribhāṣā simultaneously denies that phonemes are momentary. It affirms that the Veda recited by one person is identical to that recited by another (“ya eva vedo devadattenādhītaḥ, sa eva mayāpi”), invoking the same pratyabhijñā reasoning Shankaracharya employs.

The apparent tension resolves once the discussion is separated into distinct levels. Shankaracharya’s bhāṣya addresses the level of the eternal phoneme-type. The Paribhāṣā’s denial of eternity concerns the Veda as a structured whole across cosmic time.

pauruṣeyatva

A further difficulty arises: if the Veda originates (utpattimat) each cycle, does it become pauruṣeya, authored by Īśvara, undermining the apauruṣeyatva the Mīmāṃsaka position requires? The Paribhāṣā addresses this by redefining pauruṣeyatva.

Pauruṣeyatva is defined not as mere utterance by a person, nor as origination dependent on a person, but as utterance not modeled on a prior utterance of the same kind. Īśvara reproduces the Veda each cycle in the exact prior sequence, hence it remains apauruṣeya.

The Paribhāṣā’s “the Veda is not eternal” and Shankaracharya’s “nitya śabda” are accordingly not competing positions but a single doctrine, articulated at two levels of granularity across several centuries of scholastic refinement.
