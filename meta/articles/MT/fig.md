## Intro

> Wandering fig trees: a Beja sacred tree tradition in ancient Egypt  
Source: [TW](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-06278-6)


This is an interesting convergence of holy figs and Acacia in the Afro-Asiatic tradition with that seen in Hindu tradition. 

The quartet of holy figs -- ashvattha (pipal), nyagrodha (banyan), audumbara (_Ficus racemosa_) and plakSha (_Ficus lacor/F. microcarpa_), along with a hardwood _Acacia_ (khadira) – emerge as important in the later Vedic tradition. They are also termed chaitya-vR^ikSha-s or shrine trees in the latest Vedic/transitional tradition. 

We have evidence from material depictions, charcoal and microscopic remains that the mysterious Harappans were abundantly aware of ashvattha, nyagrodha, and probably audumbara. 

## RV vs YV
In stark contrast, in the R^igveda, no fig is mentioned in books 2-9. Only the ashvattha is mentioned once in book 1 and book 10. In sharp contrast to the situation in the RV, the two extant atharvaveda and the yajurveda saMhitA-s mention all four figs one to several times, sometimes together. 

Importantly, they refer to medicinal preparations, utensils and fire-sticks made from them – they are a commonplace aspect of their lived experience. This matches the prevalence of at least a subset of them in the Harappan horizon. 

## Early RV absence
Taken together, this suggests that the core RV was composed in regions where the classical tetrad of figs was rare or unknown, and that only in the latest RV had knowledge of them (strictly, only one) emerged. 

These figs have distinct obligate pollinating Agaonid wasps that do not survive the cold weather of the latitudes north of the highlands of what is today Afghanistan. This is yet another point in support of our contention (contra mainstream indology) that the core (older layers) RV was most likely composed early in the steppe rather than the Harappan occupation zone or inner India as commonly believed. 

## Invasion
Conversely, we see the rise in prominence of the fig-tetrad as a marker of the Aryan settlement of the Indian subcontinent. It is likely they had a certain religious significance in the Harappan world, as indicated by the suggestive depictions on seals and other artifacts. 

We posit that some of this was worked into the Aryan tradition after the admixture of the two groups. This provides a temporal marker for the composition of what we term the middle (late Bronze age) to late (early Iron age) layers of the atharvaveda and yajurveda texts. 

## Afroasiatic parallel
What is interesting in the Afroasiatic situation described in this work is: 

1. how convergently the native African figs acquired religious significance. 
2. How this significance was transferred seamlessly between cultures, closely paralleling the Harappan to Aryan transfer in India. 
3. Long persistence over religious transformations: this is also seen in India, where the chaitya-vR^ikSha, likely of old Harappan provenance, passing through Hindu tradition, was reinterpreted in the bauddha-mata.
