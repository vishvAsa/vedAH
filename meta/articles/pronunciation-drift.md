Although Vedic recitation likely represents the "oldest" continuously transmitted oral textual tradition, the Saṃhitā-Pāṭha of the Ṛgveda reflects an early standardization which started during the Kuru period and subsequently underwent orthoepic diaskeuasis/revision prior to Śākalya.

## Meter
Modern Vedic recitation is very close to the original recitation at the time of composition, albeit not identical.

For example:

• "svàr ṇá dīded aruṣéṇa bhānúnā" is metrically reconstructed as:
"súᵛar ṇá dīded aruṣéṇa bhānúnā" (Jágatī metre)

• "kvà svid āsāṃ katamā́ purāṇī́" is metrically reconstructed as:
"kúᵛa svid āsāṃ katamā́ purāṇī́" (Triṣṭúbh metre)

• "dvā́v imaú vā́tau vātaḥ" is metrically reconstructed as:
"duᵛā́v imaú vā́tau vātaḥ" (Anuṣṭúbh metre)

• "syonā́d ā́ vaḫ pratibúdhyamānāḥ" is metrically reconstructed as:
"siʸonā́d ā́ vaḫ pratibúdhyamānāḥ" (Triṣṭúbh metre)

• "jániṣṭa hí jényo ágre áhnām" is metrically reconstructed as:
"jániṣṭa hí jéniʸo ágre áhnām" (Triṣṭúbh metre)

• "Índrāgnī Vṛtrahaṇā juṣéthām" is metrically reconstructed as:
"Índrāägnī Vṛtrahaṇā juṣéthām" (Triṣṭúbh metre)

• "sahasrārghám iḍó átra bhāgám" is metrically reconstructed as
"sahasraärghám iḍó átra bhāgám" (Triṣṭúbh metre)

## Codification

The phonetic, accentual, and orthoepical features of Vedic recitation were codified early and safeguarded via conventions established by early grammarians and phoneticians (these prescriptions were later compiled in the śikṣās and prātiśākhyas) as well as via checksum-esque mnemonic techniques (i.e. vikṛtipāṭhas).

However, they were not strictly codified at the time of composition.
Nevertheless, the fidelity of transmission is very impressive.

## Comparison
### Zoroastrian
For comparison, the original composition of the Zoroastrian Gathas was broadly synchronous with the Vedas, yet the transmitted liturgical pronunciation of the Gathas (and the Avesta as a whole) reflects a much later stage of priestly transmission which was codified during the Sasanian period, "tainted" with numerous innovations such as anticipatory vowel epenthesis (e.g. Avestan nāirī- vs. Vedic nā́rī-, Avestan hauruua- vs Vedic sárva-, etc.) and a large number of scribal variants in the transcription of a single word.

### Chinese
Similarly, despite the remarkable continuity of Chinese culture, the phonology of Modern Chinese languages has changed dramatically from the Zhōu era and many of the original rhyme correspondences in the Shījīng no longer work in modern Chinese languages (Mandarin, Wú, Hakkâ, Jìn, Mǐnnán, Mǐnběi, Yuè, Xiāng, Gàn, Huī, Pínghuà, etc.)

So even if a Qīng dynasty official memorized the Shījīng, Yìjīng, Shūjīng, etc. by heart, he wouldn't be able to recognize the same Classics pronounced in the original Old Chinese.

## Fidelity level
When a child sings "sáṃ gacchadhvaṃ sáṃ vadadhvaṃ sáṃ vo mánāṃsi jānatām," it is fair to say that it reflects a very archaic pronunciation, though not necessarily the original pronunciation at time of composition (e.g. "sáṃ gacchadhvam" might've originally been "sáṃ gaścadhvam," cf. Greek "báskesthe").

Note that Sanskrit -dhvam is nearly unchanged from Proto-Indo-Iranian *-dhu̯am.
This affix becomes *-dwam in Proto-Iranic, yielding -dūm in Old Avestan via contraction and -δβəm in Young Avestan via spirantization.

## pada-pATha
In some places the padapāṭha preserves the older reading, but overall the padapāṭha feels more consciously "regularized" (and hence innovative) than the saṃhitāpāṭha.

Not only does Śākalya undo the final vowel lengthening by dvyaco 'tas tiṅaḥ, nipātasya ca, etc. but he also often undoes tujādi abhyāsadīrghatva (albeit inconsistently).
